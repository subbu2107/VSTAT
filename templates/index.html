How does valuation work?

There are two methods to calculate the Intrinsic Value of a stock: DCF Valuation and Relative Valuation. We take the average of these two methods to estimate the intrinsic value as accurately as possible

What is DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)?

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) is a valuation method used to estimate the intrinsic value of an investment, business, or asset based on its expected future cash flows. It works by projecting how much cash the asset will generate in the future and then discounting those cash flows back to their present value using a discount rate (usually based on the cost of capital or required rate of return)

In VSTAT intrinsic value calculation using the Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) method. For most companies, it uses a Free Cash Flow (FCF)-based approach, where the past 5–10 years of FCF data are collected, an average FCF is calculated, and a reasonable growth rate is estimated based on historical trends. Future FCFs are then projected for the next 10 years and discounted back to their present value using the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC). A terminal value is added to account for long-term growth beyond the projection period. For financial companies, where FCF may not be meaningful, the code uses an EPS-based DCF, projecting future earnings per share with a constant growth rate and discounting them using WACC. In both cases, the total present value is divided by the number of shares to estimate the intrinsic value per share, which is compared to the current market price to determine if the stock is undervalued or overvalued.

What is Relative valuation?

In VSTAT relative valuation of a stock by comparing its valuation multiples (P/E and P/B) with its own historical averages and those of its sector peers. The process starts by identifying the stock's sector and mapping it to a predefined peer group. The system then retrieves the stock's Earnings Per Share (EPS) and Book Value Per Share (BVPS) to calculate its own 10-year average P/E and P/B ratios based on monthly historical prices. Using these averages, it estimates two intrinsic values: one based on average P/E × EPS and another based on average P/B × BVPS

Simultaneously, it fetches sector average P/E and P/B ratios from its peer companies and calculates two more intrinsic values using sector multiples. These four valuations are then combined using weighted averages to estimate a final fair value per share. It is corrected using a simple average of the stock's own P/E and P/B-based values. The system finally compares the fair value with the current price to determine if the stock is undervalued or overvalued, returning a clear verdict along with key valuation metrics
